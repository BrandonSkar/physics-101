    Speed

    Is speeding actually worth it?

    📍 The situation

    You're driving 300 miles to visit family. Your cousin, who has a lead foot, swears they "make great time" by doing 80 while everyone else does 70.

    Is the extra speed (and the speeding-ticket risk) really buying them much? Let's do the math your cousin never did.

    💡 The big idea

    Speed is how much distance you cover in a certain amount of time.

    speed = distance ÷ timev = d ÷ t

    Like any equation, you can rearrange it to find whichever piece you're missing:

    • distance = speed × time: 2 hours at 60 mph covers 120 miles.
    • time = distance ÷ speed: 120 miles at 60 mph takes 2 hours.

    Average speed counts everything, including red lights, the drive-thru and the gas station: total distance ÷ total time.

    Physics word: velocity is speed plus a direction. "60 mph north" is a velocity. Going around a curve at a steady 60 mph, your speed stays the same but your velocity changes, because your direction does.

    Unit tip: to turn hours into minutes, multiply by 60. So 0.25 hours is 15 minutes.

    🧪 Lab: The road-trip race

    Set the trip length and both speeds, then race. Watch how little time the extra speed buys, especially on short trips.

    ✏️ Your turn

    Grandma lives 300 miles away. Including one snack stop, you average 60 mph. How many hours does the drive take?

    You know the distance and the speed. Use time = distance ÷ speed.

    time = 300 mi ÷ 60 mph = 5 hours.

    Same 300-mile trip. You drive 70 mph and your cousin drives 80 mph. How many minutes sooner does your cousin arrive?

    Find each driver's time in hours, subtract, then multiply by 60 to get minutes.

    You: 300 ÷ 70 = 4.29 h. Cousin: 300 ÷ 80 = 3.75 h. The difference is 0.54 h, and 0.54 × 60 = about 32 minutes. Not nothing, but that's 300 miles at 10 over the limit the whole way.

    Your commute is 12 miles of highway. How many minutes do you save by driving 75 mph instead of 65 mph?

    Same steps as before: the time at each speed, subtract, then × 60.

    12 ÷ 65 = 0.1846 h, and 12 ÷ 75 = 0.1600 h. The difference is 0.0246 h, and × 60 that's about 1.5 minutes. One song's worth of time, and only if traffic lets you hold 75 the whole way.

    You drive 60 miles to the lake at 30 mph (traffic!), then 60 miles home at 60 mph. What was your average speed for the whole trip?

    • 45 mph
    • 40 mph
    • 50 mph
    • 42 mph

    Average speed = total distance ÷ total time. How long did each half take?

    There: 60 ÷ 30 = 2 h. Back: 60 ÷ 60 = 1 h. That's 120 miles in 3 hours, so 40 mph. It isn't 45, because you spent twice as long going slow.

    ✅ Takeaway

    Speeding saves less time than it feels like, and the savings shrink the faster you already go. On a typical commute it's a minute or two. If you're late, leaving five minutes earlier beats driving 10 mph faster.

    Acceleration

    Can you stop in time?

    📍 The situation

    It's dusk on a country road. A deer steps out about 50 meters ahead (half a football field). Whether you stop in time comes down to three things: how fast you're going, how quickly you react and what the road is like.

    💡 The big idea

    Acceleration is how quickly your velocity changes.

    acceleration = change in velocity ÷ timea = Δv ÷ t

    A car that goes from 0 to 27 m/s (60 mph) in 6 seconds accelerates at 4.5 m/s²: it gains 4.5 m/s of speed every second. Braking is the same thing in reverse, which we call deceleration.

    Your stopping distance comes in two parts:

    • Reaction distance = speed × reaction time. While your brain notices the deer and your foot moves, you're still going full speed.
    • Braking distance = speed² ÷ (2 × deceleration). Good tires on dry pavement can slow you by about 7 m/s². Wet roads, about 4. Ice, about 1.
    stopping distance = v × treact + v² ÷ 2a

    See that speed²? Double your speed and your braking distance gets four times longer. That's why a little extra speed makes such a big difference in a crash.

    Unit tip: physics formulas want meters and seconds. To turn mph into m/s, multiply by 0.447 (or roughly divide by 2.2).

    🧪 Lab: The deer test

    Pick your speed, how alert you are and the road. Then hit the brakes and see if you stop before the deer. The yellow bar is reaction distance, the red bar is braking distance.

    ✏️ Your turn

    A car goes from 0 to 27 m/s (that's 60 mph) in 9 seconds. What's its acceleration?

    acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time.

    a = (27 − 0) m/s ÷ 9 s = 3 m/s². Every second, it gains 3 m/s of speed.

    You're cruising at 25 m/s (about 56 mph) and glance at a text for 2 seconds. How far does your car travel while you're not looking?

    Your speed doesn't change while you look away. distance = speed × time.

    d = 25 m/s × 2 s = 50 m. Half a football field, driving blind.

    You're going 20 m/s and your brakes can slow you at 8 m/s². What's your braking distance once you hit the pedal?

    braking distance = speed² ÷ (2 × deceleration).

    d = 20² ÷ (2 × 8) = 400 ÷ 16 = 25 m.

    You double your speed, from 30 mph to 60 mph. What happens to your braking distance?

    • It stays the same
    • It doubles
    • It gets 4 times longer
    • It gets 8 times longer

    Look at the formula: the speed is squared.

    Braking distance depends on speed². (2 × speed)² = 4 × speed², so the braking distance gets 4 times longer. (Your reaction distance only doubles.)

    ✅ Takeaway

    Speed and distraction both make you need more road to stop, but speed hurts squared. A phone in your hand turns a one-second reaction into a three-second one, which at highway speed is about 80 meters of driving blind.

    Gravity & free fall

    Catch it! (Your phone, that is)

    📍 The situation

    Your phone slips out of your hand. You have less than a second before it hits the floor. How long, exactly? And are your reflexes fast enough to catch it?

    💡 The big idea

    Near Earth's surface, gravity speeds up every falling object by about 9.8 m/s every second. We call that number g.

    g = 9.8 m/s²

    Galileo's big insight: heavy and light things fall at the same rate, as long as air isn't in the way. In 1971, astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather on the Moon, where there's no air, and they landed together.

    For something dropped from rest:

    distance fallen = ½ × g × t²speed = g × tor, from a height h: v = √(2 g h)

    Because time is squared, falling things pick up ground fast: 5 cm in the first 0.1 second, 20 cm by 0.2 seconds and 44 cm by 0.3 seconds.

    About air: on Earth, air pushes back harder the faster you go. A skydiver stops speeding up at around 120 mph (their terminal velocity), and a feather barely speeds up at all.

    🧪 Lab: The ruler-drop reaction test

    A classic: a friend dangles a meter stick between your fingers and lets go, and you catch it. How far it fell tells you your reaction time, using t = √(2d ÷ g). Press Get ready, then click, tap or hit the space bar the instant the ruler drops.

    ✏️ Your turn

    Your phone slips out of your hand 1.5 m above the floor. How long until it hits? (Ignore air resistance.)

    Start with d = ½ g t² and solve for t: t = √(2d ÷ g).

    t = √(2 × 1.5 ÷ 9.8) = √0.306 = 0.55 s. Your reaction time is around 0.2 s, so catching it is possible, but only just.

    How fast is that phone going when it hits the floor?

    Use speed = g × t with the time from the last problem, or v = √(2 g h).

    v = 9.8 × 0.553 = 5.4 m/s, about 12 mph. That's why phone cases exist.

    You drop a rock into an old well and hear the splash 3 seconds later. About how deep is the well? (Ignore the time the sound takes to come back up.)

    distance = ½ × g × t².

    d = ½ × 9.8 × 3² = 4.9 × 9 = 44 m. (The splash sound takes about 0.13 s to climb back up, so the real well is a bit shallower.)

    You drop a bowling ball and a soccer ball off the same balcony at the same moment. Ignoring air resistance, which hits the ground first?

    • The bowling ball, because it's heavier
    • The soccer ball, because it's lighter
    • They land at the same time
    • It depends on the height

    Does g depend on mass?

    Gravity speeds up everything by the same 9.8 m/s², whatever its mass. They land together. (In real air, from a tall enough height, the soccer ball would fall a little behind.)

    ✅ Takeaway

    Everything falls the same way: 9.8 m/s faster every second. Time is squared in the distance formula, so a fall twice as long covers four times the height.

    Projectile motion

    Nothing but net

    📍 The situation

    Every free throw, every water-balloon toss and every stream from a garden hose traces the same curve, called a parabola. There's one trick that makes all of them easy to figure out.

    💡 The big idea

    Split the motion into two parts that completely ignore each other:

    • Sideways: once the ball leaves your hand, nothing pushes it sideways, so it moves at a steady speed. distance = speed × time.
    • Up and down: gravity works on it exactly like your falling phone, 9.8 m/s² downward.

    Time is the only thing the two parts share. Find the time from one, and use it in the other.

    Weird but true: a bullet fired perfectly level and a bullet dropped from the same height hit the ground at the same time. The TV show MythBusters tested it, and the two landed almost exactly together.

    On flat ground with no air resistance, a 45° launch goes farthest:

    range = v² × sin(2θ) ÷ gat 45°: range = v² ÷ g

    🧪 Lab: Free-throw line

    The rim is 3.05 m (10 feet) off the floor and about 4.2 m away. Pick your launch angle and speed, and shoot. Tip: arcs between about 45° and 55° give the ball the biggest target.

    ✏️ Your turn

    At the same moment, you throw one ball straight out sideways and simply drop another from the same height. Which hits the ground first?

    • The thrown ball
    • The dropped ball
    • They hit at the same time

    Does moving sideways change how fast something falls?

    The sideways motion doesn't affect the falling. Both start with no downward speed and fall with the same g, so they land together. The thrown one just lands farther away.

    You kick a soccer ball at 20 m/s at a 45° angle. On flat ground with no air resistance, how far does it go?

    At 45°, range = v² ÷ g.

    Range = 20² ÷ 9.8 = 400 ÷ 9.8 = about 41 m. (Real air makes real kicks a bit shorter.)

    A marble rolls off a 1.25 m tall table at 2 m/s. How far from the table's edge does it land?

    First, find how long it takes to fall 1.25 m: t = √(2h ÷ g). Then sideways distance = speed × time.

    Falling: t = √(2 × 1.25 ÷ 9.8) = 0.505 s. Sideways: 2 m/s × 0.505 s = about 1.0 m.

    A basketball player jumps 0.6 m straight up. What's their total hang time, up and back down?

    Going up takes the same time as coming down. Find the time to fall 0.6 m, then double it.

    Down: t = √(2 × 0.6 ÷ 9.8) = 0.35 s. Up takes the same, so the hang time is 0.70 s. Even the best dunkers rarely hang for much more than a second.

    ✅ Takeaway

    Sideways motion and up-and-down motion are independent. Solve them separately, and connect them with time.

    Newton's laws

    Why you feel heavy in elevators

    📍 The situation

    Stand on a bathroom scale in an elevator (people really do this) and the number jumps around as the elevator starts and stops. Is your weight changing? Isaac Newton can explain, with three laws.

    💡 The big idea

    1. Inertia. Things keep doing what they're doing, whether that's sitting still or moving at a steady speed in a straight line, until a force makes them change. That's why your body keeps going forward when the bus brakes, and why seatbelts matter.
    2. Force = mass × acceleration. The more mass something has, the more force it takes to change its motion. One newton (N) of force speeds up 1 kg by 1 m/s². Your weight is the force of gravity on you: W = m × g.
    3. Every push has an equal push back. Push on a wall and the wall pushes on you. A rocket pushes exhaust down, and the exhaust pushes the rocket up.
    F = m × aW = m × g

    Here's the secret about scales: they don't measure your mass. They measure how hard they have to push up on you. When the elevator speeds up going up, the floor has to push harder, so you read heavier:

    scale force = m × (g + a)a is positive when speeding up going up

    History: in 1854, Elisha Otis stood on an elevator platform high above a crowd in New York and had the rope cut. His safety brake caught it. Every elevator since has one.

    🧪 Lab: The elevator scale

    Ride up and down and watch the scale. Then, for science, cut the cable. (The emergency brakes work, just like in real elevators.)

    ✏️ Your turn

    Your car stalls and you push it. The car's mass is 1,500 kg. Ignoring friction, how much force does it take to give it an acceleration of 0.2 m/s²?

    F = m × a.

    F = 1,500 × 0.2 = 300 N, about the same as lifting a 30 kg bag of dog food.

    What's the weight (the pull of gravity, in newtons) of a 70 kg person?

    W = m × g.

    W = 70 × 9.8 = 686 N. Mass (kg) is how much stuff you're made of; weight (N) is how hard gravity pulls on it.

    A 60 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator that's speeding up while going up, at 2 m/s². What does the scale read in kg? (Scales show force ÷ 9.8.)

    The floor pushes with m × (g + a). Divide that by 9.8 to get the scale's reading.

    Force = 60 × (9.8 + 2) = 708 N, and 708 ÷ 9.8 = 72.2 kg. You didn't gain 12 kg. The floor is just pushing harder to speed you up.

    Your bus brakes hard and you lurch forward. Why?

    • The brakes push you forward
    • Your body keeps moving forward because of inertia
    • Gravity tilts forward when the bus stops
    • The air in the bus pushes you

    Which of Newton's laws is about things that keep doing what they were doing?

    Newton's first law. The bus slows down, but nothing has slowed you down yet, so you keep going forward until a seat, a pole or a seatbelt pushes back.

    ✅ Takeaway

    It takes a force to change motion, not to keep it going. A scale reads how hard it pushes on you, which is why it reads more when you speed up going up, less going down and zero in free fall.

    Momentum

    The egg drop

    📍 The situation

    Why do airbags save lives? Why do you bend your knees when you jump off a wall? Why does a dropped phone survive the couch but not the sidewalk? It's all the same idea.

    💡 The big idea

    Momentum is mass in motion. A loaded truck at 30 mph has far more of it than a bike at 30 mph.

    momentum = mass × velocityp = m × v

    To stop something, you have to take away all of its momentum. The force you need depends on how much time you take to do it:

    force × time = change in momentumF × Δt = Δp (called impulse)

    The change in momentum is set by how fast you were going. But you get to choose the time. Make the stop last 10 times longer and the force drops 10 times. That's the job of airbags, crumple zones, bike helmets, gym mats and bent knees.

    Conservation of momentum: in a collision or a push-off, the total momentum before equals the total after. Push off a friend on ice skates and you both slide away, with the lighter person going faster.

    🧪 Lab: Egg drop

    Choose a height and something to land on. Softer things stop the egg over a longer time, so the force on it is smaller. An eggshell cracks at somewhere around 30 newtons.

    ✏️ Your turn

    What's the momentum of a 1,200 kg car going 25 m/s?

    p = m × v.

    p = 1,200 × 25 = 30,000 kg·m/s.

    In a crash, a 70 kg passenger moving at 15 m/s is stopped by an airbag in 0.15 s. What's the average force on them?

    F × Δt = Δp, so F = (m × v) ÷ Δt.

    Δp = 70 × 15 = 1,050 kg·m/s. F = 1,050 ÷ 0.15 = 7,000 N. Hitting a hard dashboard in 0.01 s instead would mean 105,000 N, 15 times more.

    On an ice rink, an 80 kg parent and a 40 kg kid stand still, then push off each other. The kid slides away at 2 m/s. How fast does the parent slide the other way?

    The total momentum starts at zero, so the two must cancel out: m₁ × v₁ = m₂ × v₂.

    Kid: 40 × 2 = 80 kg·m/s. Parent: 80 × v = 80, so v = 1 m/s. Twice the mass, half the speed.

    Why do you bend your knees when you land from a jump?

    • It makes your momentum smaller before you land
    • It makes the stop take longer, so the force is smaller
    • It makes gravity weaker
    • It doesn't matter, it's just a habit

    You can't change how fast you're going when you land. What can you change?

    Your momentum when you land is set by how high you jumped. Bending your knees stretches out the stopping time, and since F = Δp ÷ Δt, a longer stop means a smaller force on your joints.

    ✅ Takeaway

    Stopping takes the same change in momentum however you do it. Spread it over more time and the force goes down. Almost every piece of safety gear you own is a time-stretcher.

    Energy

    Burn off that donut

    📍 The situation

    A glazed donut has about 250 Calories. You decide to burn it off by taking the stairs at work. How many floors is that? (Brace yourself.)

    💡 The big idea

    Energy is what it takes to make things happen. It's never created or destroyed; it just changes form. We measure it in joules (J).

    • Work = force × distance. Lifting a 10 kg box 1 m takes about 98 J.
    • Potential energy is energy stored by height: PE = m × g × h.
    • Kinetic energy is energy of motion: KE = ½ × m × v².
    PE = m g hKE = ½ m v²

    A roller coaster trades one for the other. At the bottom of a drop, the height energy has all turned into speed, so m g h = ½ m v², which gives v = √(2 g h). That's the same formula as a falling phone.

    About food: one food Calorie (with a capital C) is 4,184 J. Your muscles are only about 25% efficient, and the rest of the energy turns into heat. That's why exercise makes you sweat.

    🧪 Lab: Stairs vs. snacks

    Pick a snack and set your weight. We'll work out how many floors of stairs (3 m each) it takes to use up its energy, counting your muscles' 25% efficiency.

    ✏️ Your turn

    You lift a 20 kg suitcase 1.5 m up into your car's trunk. How much energy (work) did that take?

    Lifting stores energy as PE = m × g × h.

    PE = 20 × 9.8 × 1.5 = 294 J.

    A roller coaster starts from rest at the top of a 50 m drop. Ignoring friction, how fast is it going at the bottom?

    All the PE turns into KE: m g h = ½ m v², so v = √(2 g h).

    v = √(2 × 9.8 × 50) = √980 = 31 m/s, about 70 mph. Notice that the mass canceled out: a full train and an empty one reach the same speed.

    What's the kinetic energy of a 1,000 kg car going 20 m/s?

    KE = ½ × m × v². Square the speed first.

    KE = ½ × 1,000 × 20² = 500 × 400 = 200,000 J. To stop, your brakes have to turn all of that into heat.

    Which has more kinetic energy: a 1,000 kg car going 20 m/s, or a 2,000 kg truck going 10 m/s?

    • The car
    • The truck
    • They're the same

    Work out ½ m v² for each. Which one gets squared?

    Car: ½ × 1,000 × 20² = 200,000 J. Truck: ½ × 2,000 × 10² = 100,000 J. The car has twice as much, because speed is squared and mass isn't.

    ✅ Takeaway

    Energy only changes form: food into work and heat, height into speed, speed into brake heat. And food holds a lot of it. Exercise is great for you, but it's a slow way to burn off a donut.

    Power & electricity

    Why is my power bill so high?

    📍 The situation

    You plug a little space heater in under your desk to keep your toes warm. Next month, your electric bill jumps by $60. How can something so small cost so much?

    💡 The big idea

    Power is how fast energy gets used. It's measured in watts (W): 1 watt is 1 joule every second.

    power = energy ÷ timeP = E ÷ t

    Your power company bills you in kilowatt-hours (kWh): 1 kWh is 1,000 watts running for 1 hour.

    cost = kilowatts × hours × price per kWh

    Anything that makes heat (space heaters, dryers, ovens, hair dryers, kettles) is a power hog. Phones, laptops and LED bulbs barely sip.

    Voltage (volts, V) is how hard electricity is pushed, and current (amps, A) is how much of it flows. Multiply them to get power:

    power = voltage × currentP = V × I

    In your house: US outlets are 120 V, and most circuits have a 15 A breaker. That's about 1,800 W before the breaker trips to keep the wires from overheating. It's why space heaters almost all top out at 1,500 W.

    🧪 Lab: Your power bill

    Set how many hours a day you use each thing, and see what it adds to a 30-day bill. Then try running the whole kitchen on one circuit.

    ✏️ Your turn

    A 1,500 W space heater runs 8 hours a day for 30 days. Electricity costs $0.17 per kWh. How much does it add to your bill?

    Turn watts into kilowatts first (1,500 W = 1.5 kW). Then cost = kW × hours × price.

    Energy: 1.5 kW × 8 h × 30 days = 360 kWh. Cost: 360 × $0.17 = $61.20, for one little heater.

    A 1,200 W microwave plugs into a 120 V outlet. How much current does it draw?

    P = V × I, so I = P ÷ V.

    I = 1,200 ÷ 120 = 10 A. That's two-thirds of a typical 15 A circuit, all by itself.

    You swap a 60 W bulb for a 9 W LED that's just as bright. It's on 5 hours a day. At $0.17 per kWh, how much do you save in a year?

    The savings come from the difference, 60 − 9 = 51 W. Work out the kWh in a year, then multiply by the price.

    51 W × 5 h × 365 days = 93,075 Wh, or 93.1 kWh. Then 93.1 × $0.17 = about $15.80 a year, for one bulb. Swap 20 bulbs and it's over $300.

    Your 1,800 W hair dryer and 1,100 W microwave are on the same 120 V circuit with a 15 A breaker. What happens if you run both at once?

    • Nothing, it's fine
    • The breaker trips, because together they need about 24 A
    • The microwave runs at half power
    • The outlet switches to 240 V

    Add up the watts, then find the current with I = P ÷ V.

    The total is 2,900 W, and 2,900 ÷ 120 = 24 A. That's way over 15 A, so click: the breaker trips. It's a safety switch that keeps the wires in your walls from overheating.

    ✅ Takeaway

    To guess what something costs, look at its watts and how long it runs. Heat-makers are the hogs. And if your breaker keeps tripping, add up the watts on that circuit: past about 1,800 W, it's the physics, not bad luck.

    Waves & sound

    How far away is that storm?

    📍 The situation

    Flash! You start counting: one-Mississippi, two-Mississippi… BOOM. Your grandpa always said to count the seconds and divide by five to get the distance in miles. Was he right?

    💡 The big idea

    The light from the lightning reaches you almost instantly: it covers 300,000 km every second. Sound is much slower, about 343 m/s in air. That's roughly 1 km every 3 seconds, or 1 mile every 5 seconds. Grandpa was right.

    distance = speed of sound × timecount ÷ 5 ≈ miles · count ÷ 3 ≈ km

    Sound is a wave of pressure moving through the air. Every wave follows the same rule:

    speed = frequency × wavelengthv = f × λ

    Frequency (in hertz, Hz) is how many waves pass by each second. You hear it as pitch: more waves per second sounds higher.

    The Doppler effect: when an ambulance drives toward you, each wave starts a little closer to you than the last, so the waves bunch up and the siren sounds higher. As it drives away, the waves spread out and it sounds lower. That "neee-yowww" is physics. Police radar and weather radar use the same trick with radio waves.

    Safety: if you can hear thunder, you're close enough to be struck. Head inside and wait 30 minutes after the last rumble.

    🧪 Lab: Count the thunder

    Turn your sound on. When the lightning flashes, a timer starts. Press I heard it! (or the space bar) when the thunder reaches you, and see how close your distance is.

    🧪 Lab: The Doppler drive-by

    Send an ambulance past you and listen to the pitch drop. The circles are its sound waves. Watch them bunch up in front and stretch out behind.

    ✏️ Your turn

    You see lightning and count 6 seconds until the thunder. Sound travels at 343 m/s. How far away was the strike?

    distance = speed × time.

    d = 343 × 6 = 2,058 m, about 2 km or 1.3 miles. Divide-by-5 gives 1.2 miles and divide-by-3 gives 2 km, which is close enough to know it's time to head inside.

    The note A that orchestras tune to has a frequency of 440 Hz. With sound at 343 m/s, what's its wavelength?

    v = f × λ, so λ = v ÷ f.

    λ = 343 ÷ 440 = 0.78 m. Each wave of that note is about as long as your arm.

    You shout toward a canyon wall and hear the echo 2 seconds later. How far away is the wall?

    The sound has to get there and come back.

    In 2 s, sound travels 343 × 2 = 686 m. That's a round trip, so the wall is 343 m away. Bats and ships' sonar use echoes in exactly this way.

    An ambulance with its siren on is driving away from you. Compared with the siren's real pitch, what do you hear?

    • A higher pitch
    • A lower pitch
    • The same pitch, just quieter

    Moving away: do the waves bunch up or spread out?

    Driving away stretches the waves out, so fewer reach you each second: a lower pitch. That's the "yowww" at the end of "neee-yowww".

    ✅ Takeaway

    Light is basically instant and sound isn't, so the gap between them tells you the distance. Waves carry pitch as frequency, and anything moving toward you squeezes its waves into a higher pitch.

    Final exam

    The final exam

    📋 The rules

    Ten questions from across the whole course. You get one try at each and there are no hints, but the 🧮 calculator and 📐 formula sheet are allowed. Each right answer is worth 20 XP. Finish all ten to unlock your diploma.

    ✏️ The questions

    You walk 3 km in 45 minutes. What's your average speed in km/h?

    45 minutes is 0.75 hours, and 3 ÷ 0.75 = 4 km/h.

    You're going 30 m/s, and it takes you 1.5 s to react to brake lights ahead. How far do you travel before your foot even touches the brake?

    Reaction distance = 30 × 1.5 = 45 m.

    A water balloon is dropped off a 20 m tall roof. How long until it splashes? (Ignore air.)

    t = √(2 × 20 ÷ 9.8) = √4.08 = 2.0 s.

    You toss a ball straight up. At the very top, for an instant, it isn't moving. What's its acceleration right then?

    • Zero
    • 9.8 m/s² downward
    • 9.8 m/s² upward
    • It depends on how hard you threw it

    Gravity never switches off. Even at the top, the ball is speeding up downward at 9.8 m/s², which is exactly why it doesn't stay up there.

    A 50 kg skateboarder gets a net push of 100 N. What's their acceleration?

    a = F ÷ m = 100 ÷ 50 = 2 m/s².

    Car crumple zones make crashes safer because they:

    • Make the car heavier
    • Make the collision last longer, which lowers the force
    • Get rid of the car's momentum before the crash
    • Make the car bounce off whatever it hits

    F = Δp ÷ Δt. Crumpling stretches out the time of the crash, so the force on the people inside goes down.

    A 0.145 kg baseball is pitched at 40 m/s (about 90 mph). What's its kinetic energy?

    KE = ½ × 0.145 × 40² = 0.0725 × 1,600 = 116 J.

    A 500 W gaming PC runs 4 hours a day for 30 days. At $0.20 per kWh, what does that cost?

    0.5 kW × 4 h × 30 days = 60 kWh, and 60 × $0.20 = $12.

    You count 9 seconds between a flash and its thunder. About how many miles away was the lightning? (Sound travels 343 m/s, and 1 mile is about 1,609 m.)

    343 × 9 = 3,087 m, and 3,087 ÷ 1,609 = 1.9 miles. The quick rule (9 ÷ 5 = 1.8) gets you close.

    Astronauts float around inside the space station. Why?

    • There's no gravity in space
    • They're in free fall, falling around Earth along with the station
    • The station's air holds them up
    • They're too far from the Sun

    At the station's height, gravity is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. But the station and everyone in it are falling together, moving sideways so fast that they keep missing Earth. Like the elevator with its cable cut, free fall makes a scale read zero.
